I am a health economist who applies economic theory and causal inference to high-impact problems in U.S. health care. My work spans both the supply side, including the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) by hospitals and health systems and the effect of insurance policy on access to care, and the demand side, including mental health, suicide risk, substance use, and body image among adolescents and adults.

My research combines applied microeconomics with quasi-experimental methods, such as difference-in-differences, event studies, and instrumental variables, using large national surveys and administrative data. A core focus is how health systems adopt AI to manage resources and operations, and which providers are left behind. A second line of research examines the economics of critical minerals, where I estimate the hidden security costs of import dependence for the United States and other G10 economies.

I am a Lecturer in Economics at Rutgers University–Camden and Adjunct Faculty in the School of Business Administration at Widener University. I received my Ph.D. in Economics from the University of Tennessee, Knoxville, in December 2025.

Publications

  1. Scrolling into Substance Use: Social Media Use Frequency and Substance Use Among U.S. High School Students

    P. Prakash, F. Beltran-Silva, and A. Teotia

    Drug and Alcohol Dependence Reports, 2026. doi:10.1016/j.dadr.2026.100453

    Summary

    Using the 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey of 10,027 U.S. high school students, the first wave with a standalone measure of how often students check social media, we estimate survey-weighted linear probability models with state fixed effects. Students who check social media more than once an hour are about 19 percentage points more likely to report current alcohol use. Associations are also large for nicotine vaping and cannabis and smaller for cigarettes, and they concentrate in the two highest-frequency groups.

  2. Operational AI Adoption in U.S. Inpatient Rehabilitation Facilities: Organizational Selection and Domain Heterogeneity, 2021–2024

    A. Teotia, Z. Henley, and P. Prakash

    Archives of Physical Medicine and Rehabilitation, 2026. doi:10.1016/j.apmr.2026.08.016

    Summary

    Using American Hospital Association survey data on 276 inpatient rehabilitation facilities, we find that operational AI use more than doubled between 2021 and 2024, from 11% to 25% of facilities, led by system-affiliated, non-profit, and teaching facilities. Organizational structure explains 50–67% of the variation in adoption, more than market conditions, patient volume, and region combined. Tools for optimizing operational efficiency spread fastest, while staff-scheduling tools lag furthest behind.

  3. Social Media Use Frequency, Body Image, and Weight-Control Behavior Among U.S. High School Students

    P. Prakash, F. Beltran-Silva, and A. Teotia

    Acta Psychologica, vol. 269, 2026. doi:10.1016/j.actpsy.2026.107446

    Summary

    Using the 2023 Youth Risk Behavior Survey, this paper studies how the frequency of social media checking relates to high school students' perceptions of their weight and their weight-control behaviors.

Job market paper

Rare Earth Element Security Premiums

with Charles Sims

Revise and resubmit Resource and Energy Economics

Importing economies face rare earth supply disruptions whose costs private buyers do not weigh when they decide how much to import. Using a model of expected domestic welfare, we show that each additional unit of rare earth imports carries a social cost on top of its private cost. This security premium provides an economic rationale for policies that reduce import dependence. We estimate these premiums for G10 economies using rare earth metal and magnet trade data from 1995 to 2023. The premiums vary widely across countries, from $2.39 to $7.71 per kg for rare earth metals and from $0.46 to $30.36 per kg for magnets. For the United States, the premium on rare earth metals is larger than the premium on imported oil, and premiums may be substantially larger when importers are imperfectly informed about the supply risks they face.

Teaching philosophy

My goal is not to move students through a syllabus. It is to give them a durable way to analyze real problems, whether a policy debate in the news or a career decision five years from now.

Many of my students work, care for family members, or are the first in their families to attend college, so I design courses in which one hard week does not decide a grade.

By the end of a course, I want students to be able to read a piece of economic news and identify the mechanism behind it, build a simple model of a real-world trade-off, and explain why a policy might produce unintended consequences.

Policy briefs

These briefs translate research on rare earth element markets for policymakers. Both were published by the Baker School of Public Policy and Public Affairs at the University of Tennessee, Knoxville.

Should the U.S. Be Concerned about China’s Dominance in the Rare Earth Element Market?

J. Welch, C. Sims, P. Prakash, S. Gilpatric, and S. Holladay. Policy brief, March 2024.

China accounts for the lion’s share of the rare earth products the United States imports, a position that carries economic risks similar to OPEC’s long influence over oil. The brief argues that the United States and its allies can reduce this risk by coordinating to diversify supply, with multiple sources produced under shared environmental and labor standards.

Policy Responses to Rare Earth Element Supply Shocks

Policy brief, June 2025.

In 16 of the past 20 years, the United States imported all of the rare earth metals it consumed. The brief examines how policy can respond when that supply is disrupted.
